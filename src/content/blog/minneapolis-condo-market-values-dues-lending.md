**Minneapolis condo values dropped over the past year while houses in the same city went up. In the video above I explain the three big reasons, including a lending rule that can block regular mortgages for an entire building, even units with nothing wrong, and what to check if you own a condo or are shopping for one.**

Figures are as stated in the video, as of November 2026 when I made it.

## Prices: condos down, houses up

- The typical Minneapolis condo was worth about **$228,000** in August, down **4.2%** from a year earlier. Minneapolis single-family homes were up about **2.1%**.
- Some areas were hit harder: in the **55403 ZIP code**, on the edge of downtown, values were down about **7.4%** in one year, almost $15,000 on a $200,000 condo.
- Zoomed out, Zillow says the typical Twin Cities home is worth about **$389,000**, up about 1.8%. The whole market isn't falling; condos are.
- Minneapolis condo prices **peaked in 2021** and are still about **9% below** that peak. If you bought near the peak, you may still sell for less than you paid, before agent fees and closing costs.

## Reason 1: too much supply

Across the metro, condos have the most months' supply of any home type. Downtown Minneapolis sits at about **7.5 months**, a level we haven't seen since around 2009 or 2010, while the city as a whole, across all home types, is around **three months**. About **38%** of listings around the metro have taken a price cut, and this past winter Twin Cities condo sellers knocked about **5%** off their price on average. If you're buying a condo, negotiate hard. If you're selling, price realistically.

## Reason 2: insurance is driving up dues

Every condo building has a **master insurance policy**, paid through your association dues, and that cost has climbed fast. Some Minnesota buildings have seen premiums jump almost **400%**. One local HOA group's 2024 member survey found insurance took about **34%** of the average HOA budget, up from **27%** a couple of years before. At Windwood Condominiums in Edina, insurance jumped over 400% and dues went up overnight.

Your mortgage payment may be fixed, but your dues, insurance and property taxes aren't, and you can't refinance your way out of them. Higher dues also pull values down, because lenders count them when deciding what a buyer can borrow: at today's rates, **$262 a month in dues** is about **$38,000 less** purchasing power. And when a building comes up short, it can bill each owner directly through a **special assessment**.

## Reason 3: tighter Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac condo rules

Most regular mortgages are backed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. If they won't back a loan, most lenders won't make it. This year both tightened their condo rules:

- As of **August**, the old shortcut review is effectively gone. Buildings with **more than 10 units** need a full review of the building's finances before a regular loan can be approved.
- The master policy **deductible is capped at $50,000 per unit**.
- **Reserves** (repair savings) must be at least **10%** of the building's budget, rising to **15% in January**, which likely means dues go up again for many buildings. If you plan to sell next year, watch for that.

If a building fails review, it can be marked **unavailable**, and regular loans are off the table for **every unit**, even spotless ones. Common reasons: not enough reserves, repairs that keep getting put off, insurance gaps, too many owners behind on dues (Fannie Mae draws the line at **15%** of units 60 or more days late) or a big lawsuit.

Then a buyer may need to pay cash, or use a special loan from a local or regional bank, which often runs **1.5 to 3 points higher** on the rate and needs **15% to 25% down**. That shrinks your buyer pool fast. Sometimes the problem isn't your unit; it's the building.

## Some good news: Minnesota's new HOA law

Minnesota passed a new HOA law this year, mostly effective **January 1**:

- Fines and late fees are capped.
- Boards need **three written bids** on projects of **$50,000** or more.
- Board members can't vote on deals they make money from.
- Boards can't punish owners for standing up for their rights.

It protects you from a bad board, but it doesn't fix insurance prices or lending rules.

## Should you buy a condo at all?

Buying a condo is like buying a Corvette: you buy it because you love driving it, not because you expect it to go up in value. If you want to live downtown, walk to dinner or a game and skip the yard work and snow shoveling, go for it, just don't count on big appreciation. (You can also skip the mowing and shoveling in a house by hiring it out.)

The mistake I see: buying a condo as a **first step to build equity** and move up. Right now, that plan doesn't work very well. Buy a condo if you want that way of living, plan to stay a while, and check the building first.

## Compare the monthly cost, not the price

Example: a **$230,000 condo** with 10% down at a rate a little over 7% is about **$1,400 a month** for the loan, before taxes and insurance. Add **$450** in dues and you're around **$1,870 a month**. That same monthly amount covers a **house up to about $300,000** with 10% down, about $70,000 more home, because there are no dues. The house has its own insurance and upkeep, but look at which one gained value last year. A **townhome** can be a good middle ground: low maintenance, more space, usually an attached garage, though many have dues too.

## Check the building before you buy or list

1. What is the building's **lending status**?
2. Ask the board for its latest **reserve study**.
3. Are any **special assessments** coming?
4. When does the **master policy** renew?

Your buyer's lender and your agent should be able to check all of it.
