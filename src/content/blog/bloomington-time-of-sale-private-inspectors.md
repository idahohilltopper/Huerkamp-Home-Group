If you're selling a house, townhome or condo in Bloomington, there's a city step not every Minnesota city requires: a Time-of-Sale housing evaluation, filed with the city before the home is offered for sale. On Monday, Nov. 30, the Bloomington City Council will hold a public hearing on a change to how that evaluation gets done. According to [the city's hearing notice](https://www.bloomingtonmn.gov/cd/news/notice-public-hearing-amending-citys-time-sale-housing-evaluation-regulations-2026-11-19), the ordinance would amend the Time-of-Sale rules "by removing the license requirement for non-city evaluators, allowing for qualified private evaluators, and adding items to be evaluated."

In plain terms: today the evaluation is done through the city. Under the proposal, a seller could ask to have a qualified private home inspector do it instead. Here's how the program works now, what the draft ordinance would change, and what it means if you're buying or selling.

## How Bloomington's Time-of-Sale evaluation works today

From [the city's Time-of-Sale program page](https://www.bloomingtonmn.gov/bldg/time-sale-tos-housing-inspection-program):

- **Who needs one:** an evaluation must be completed and a report filed with the city "for all single and two-family dwellings, condominiums, townhouses and mobile homes offered for sale." Rental buildings with four or more units are not included, and the page lists other exemptions, including new construction and sheriff's sales.
- **Cost:** "The cost for the Time of Sale inspection is $250 plus service charge fee." The city schedules the inspection, by phone at 952-563-8930 or through its online request form.
- **How long it's good for:** the report and certificate are "valid for one year from the date of issue," and only for the owner named on the report.
- **What has to be fixed:** "Only items which pose an immediate hazard to the occupants of the structure are required to be repaired." Other problems are noted in the report but not required. The page lists the immediate-hazard categories.
- **Who fixes them:** the seller, generally. If the seller can't or won't, the buyer may take on the immediate hazards in the report, with the city's written consent.
- **Energy disclosure:** since April 2022 the report has included an energy disclosure for the home.

## What the proposed ordinance would change

The draft is [posted with the notice](https://www.bloomingtonmn.gov/sites/default/files/2026-11/2026-1433%20-%20Ordinance.pdf). It amends Chapter 14 of the City Code. The main changes:

- **Private home inspectors could do the evaluation.** Evaluations "may be performed by" qualified city employees, or by "qualified private home inspectors, upon the application of the owner or owner's agent to the Building Official and approved by the Building Official."
- **Who qualifies:** a private home inspector qualifies when they show the Building Official that they are "licensed to perform time-of-sale evaluations in another Minnesota municipality," or that "a regular course of their business or employment duties consists of home inspections performed for real property sales or transactions." They, or their employer, must also carry commercial general liability insurance of at least "$1,000,000 per occurrence."
- **No separate city evaluator license.** The draft deletes the section requiring an evaluator license from the city, which expires every Dec. 31, and the section on denying or revoking those licenses. It also drops the current system where the city keeps a certified list of private contractors and decides on its own who performs each evaluation.
- **Evaluation items.** The draft amends the energy disclosure items for the stove and the dryer (fuel type) and the immediate-hazard item for "properly located operational smoke detectors and carbon monoxide detectors." It also updates "mobile home" to "manufactured home."
- **When it would start:** the draft says it "shall take effect upon its adoption and publication according to law." Nothing is final until the council votes.

What the draft does **not** spell out: whether the $250 city fee or any other fee would apply when a private inspector does the evaluation. If that matters for your sale, ask the city's building official, Bernadette Gillespie (952-563-4709), who is listed as the contact on the notice.

## What it means if you're buying or selling in Bloomington

- **Selling soon?** Nothing changes until the council adopts the ordinance and it's published. Until then, schedule the evaluation through the city as you do today, and do it early: the report must be filed before the home is offered for sale, and it's good for one year.
- **If it passes,** you could ask to use a private inspector who meets the city's requirements. That may help with timing in a busy spring market. Get the Building Official's approval before the inspection, since the draft makes approval part of the process.
- **Budget for repairs, not just the inspection.** Only immediate hazards must be fixed, but those are on the seller unless the buyer agrees, with the city's consent, to take them on. Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors are among the items the city checks, and an easy fix to handle before the evaluation.
- **Buying in Bloomington?** Read the Time-of-Sale report the seller filed, including the energy disclosure. It isn't the same as your own full home inspection, which looks at much more than immediate hazards.

## More Bloomington housing items on the calendar

- **Opportunity Housing ordinance.** The Planning Commission continues its public hearing on [Case PL2026-182](https://www.bloomingtonmn.gov/plan/news/notice-public-hearing-city-code-amendment-opportunity-housing-ordinance-2026-10-20) on Dec. 3, 2026, at 6 p.m. It would amend the city's Housing Opportunity and Preservation chapter "related to affordability requirements, building performance incentives, and updating language and formatting."
