**Minnesota doesn't have one best place to live. It has options for the best place for *you*, and that's a different question. Everyone asks me where they should live, so here are 10 places from my own experience and my clients', with what each is best for and the honest trade-off.**

## 1. Minneapolis: best for real city life

Walk around Bde Maka Ska on a Saturday morning, get coffee two blocks off the water, bike a trail in the afternoon and catch a show that night, all without a 40-minute drive. Minneapolis parks rank **third in the country**, and about 99% of residents live within a 10-minute walk of one. Neighborhoods feel very different: the North Loop is polished and central, Northeast is artsy, and Lowry Hill East feels lively.

**The trade-off:** you choose block by block, without big yards or quiet streets, and city property taxes hit your monthly payment hard. Light rail only helps if both your home and your job are on the line. For most people, life still runs on a car. See [Minneapolis](/communities/minneapolis).

## 2. Saint Paul: character without the intensity

Same metro, older homes, a slower pace. Walk Grand Avenue on a weekend and you'll see it: one-of-a-kind shops, long-running restaurants and older homes under big trees. Highland Park, Summit Hill and Mac-Groveland come up all the time. Saint Paul's parks rank **fourth in the country**.

**The trade-off:** it isn't trying to be Minneapolis after dark, and if your job and friends are west or southwest, that drive gets old. If your life leans east, you'll love it. Read our [Saint Paul pros and cons](/blog/saint-paul-pros-and-cons).

## 3. Plymouth: the practical west metro pick

Convenience without paying for the city name. It feels settled and green, with parks, trails and lakes everywhere and easy access to big stores. You can head toward Minneapolis or the west metro job centers without wrecking your day.

**The trade-off:** no cute downtown, it's not very walkable, and it can feel spread out. Prices can still be higher than people expect. See [Plymouth](/communities/plymouth).

## 4. Eden Prairie: polished convenience and airport access

Eden Prairie was clearly planned with a vision: great highway access, big employers nearby, an easy shot to the airport, and stores and parks built into the plan. Some buyers love it the same day; others find it a bit too tidy. The Green Line Extension (14.5 miles, 16 stations) ends here, with riders expected in 2027.

**The trade-off:** it's still car-dependent, so don't buy expecting to ride light rail everywhere, and it's a pricey market. You're paying for location and convenience on purpose. See [Eden Prairie](/communities/eden-prairie).

## 5. Edina: close-in living and a known address

Close to Minneapolis, with older neighborhoods under huge trees, strong city services, and shopping and dinner at 50th & France. Edina isn't automatically the best suburb in Minnesota; you go for the address and the closeness, and you pay for both.

**The trade-off:** the price tag is only half the equation. Get the real property tax bill before you write an offer, and run the monthly numbers first. No one can promise your home will go up in value. See [Edina](/communities/edina).

## 6. Maple Grove: newer homes, north metro lifestyle

Arbor Lakes on a Friday night tells you most of what you need to know: dinner, errands and a movie in one spot. Homes feel new, trails and parks run throughout, and it's still growing, with about 3,600 housing units added since 2020, the third most in the west metro. New construction is still available.

**The trade-off:** commuting to the south metro, the airport or the southeast metro can be a grind, and there's no old-fashioned downtown. It pays off if your life stays north. See [Maple Grove](/communities/maple-grove).

## 7. Woodbury: east metro space and newer neighborhoods

You know what you're getting: newer neighborhoods, newer stores, parks minutes away. Woodbury added more than 4,200 housing units since 2020, the second most in the metro.

**The trade-off:** it feels farther from Minneapolis than the map suggests, and parts are still being built out; older and newer sections feel very different, so drive both. It rewards people whose lives are on the east side. See [Woodbury](/communities/woodbury).

## 8. Wayzata and Lake Minnetonka: best for lake life

Coffee downtown, a walk by the water and dinner two blocks away is a normal Saturday in Wayzata. For people moving from the coasts, it hits hard; you don't find water living like this in most of the Midwest.

**The trade-off:** lake life is a lifestyle and a job: boats, docks in spring, crowds in July, and a different place in January. And there are rules. The **Lake Minnetonka Conservation District** and the **Minnesota DNR** both have a say on docks, shoreline work and what you can do on the water. Check dock rights, shoreline rules, septic and utilities, and flooding before your inspection period ends. See [Wayzata](/communities/wayzata) and our [lake homes page](/lake-homes).

## 9. Lakeville: new construction and south metro life

Full disclosure: I grew up here, and my parents still live here, so I'm not neutral. Lakeville added **4,900 housing units from 2020 to 2025**, the most of any city in Minnesota, and I-35 runs right through it toward Bloomington and the airport.

In 2026, Lakeville approved a **temporary pause of up to one year** on new residential plat applications. Already-approved plats aren't affected (675 single-family lots and 644 townhome units can still be built), so new construction is still available. Ask what stage your neighborhood is in before you commit to a builder.

**The trade-off:** if you work north or west, it costs you time every day, and popping into Minneapolis on a whim isn't easy. See [Lakeville](/communities/lakeville).

## 10. Rochester: health care careers and a city of its own

Rochester is its own decision. You're not commuting to the Twin Cities from here (about an hour and a half away). The city runs on **Mayo Clinic**, a huge advantage if you work in health care. There's a **$5.6 billion** plan to make Rochester a global health destination, and Mayo is in the middle of a roughly **$5 billion downtown expansion**. It has a real downtown, parks, restaurants, an international airport and the arts, without the sprawl.

**The trade-off:** the local economy leans heavily on one employer, and if you'd spend every weekend driving to the metro, it may not be for you.
