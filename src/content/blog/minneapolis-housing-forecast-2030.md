**Here's the housing forecast nobody wants to hear: how prices might move, where mortgage rates are really headed, and one number that flips the whole picture. If you're buying or selling in the next few years, you may be waiting for a market that never shows up.**

I went through the latest Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and Twin Cities numbers, as of my video above (November 2026).

## What 100-plus experts expect for prices

The **Fannie Mae and Pulsenomics Home Price Expectations Survey** (late August) gathers more than 100 economists, analysts and real estate pros. Their median forecast for national home price growth:

| Year | Forecast |
|---|---|
| 2026 | 2.5% |
| 2027 | 2.2% |
| 2028 | 2.7% |
| 2029 | 3.1% |
| 2030 | 3.3% |

That's about **14.6% over five years**. The most optimistic group sees **22.7%**; the most cautious, **6.6%**. Even the cautious group sees prices rising, not falling. **This isn't a crash forecast.**

## 2027 looks like the slow year

2.2% is the weakest year in the panel's run, and Fannie Mae's own separate forecast is even lower for 2027: about **1%**. If you're planning to sell in the next year or two, the market won't do you many favors, so pricing and preparation matter even more.

## Rates aren't coming down much

- The panel's median forecast for the **30-year fixed at the end of 2027 is 6.4%**.
- Only **7%** of the panel sees rates below 5.5%.
- Almost half raised their long-term rate outlook this year; only 3% lowered it.
- Freddie Mac's weekly average recently topped **7%**, up from about 6.3% a year earlier, and Fannie Mae expects roughly 6.7% to 6.8% through next year.
- Most of the panel expects **rate lock-in** (owners holding on to low-rate loans) to last another **three to five years**.

Plan around today's rates, not the ones you had.

## The number that flips it: inflation

None of those growth forecasts are adjusted for inflation. Non-housing inflation has recently run about **3.5% to 4.6%** a year. Pulsenomics ran the math using the panel's numbers: a five-year gain could become a **real loss of about 3.5% to 8.5%** in buying power. That's not the panel's forecast, and it's not an apples-to-apples comparison, so treat it as a gut check. It's like a raise that doesn't keep up with your grocery bill.

## Two Minnesota forces pulling in opposite directions

**1. Demographics.** About 35% of the panel named demographics and migration as the biggest force on housing over the next five years. Minnesota's 65-plus population is projected to reach about **1.23 million by 2035**, which means more downsizing, retirement moves and estate sales over time. But 68% of boomer owners say they'll likely age in place. Freddie Mac calls it "more of a tide than a tsunami," so don't expect a flood of listings.

**2. Not enough building.** The seven-county region's goal is **18,000 new housing units a year**, and it has missed that three years running. In 2025, builders **permitted 12,161 units**, down from more than 22,000 in 2021. (Permits, not finished homes.) Among 12 similar metros, we ranked seventh in permits per resident. If building stays slow, supply stays tight.

## What it could mean for Twin Cities values

The August metro median was about **$405,000**. Applying the panel's national scenarios to that starting point (an illustration, not a local forecast):

- **Cautious:** about $432,000 by 2030
- **Median:** about **$464,000**
- **Optimistic:** about $497,000

Even the slowest scenario has values growing in dollars, just slower than we got used to.

## What to do with this

- **Staying five-plus years?** Timing matters much less.
- **Might move in two or three years?** Pay attention. Closing costs, commissions, repairs and moving can eat most or all of a small gain. A longer stay spreads those costs out and lets you ride out a slow year like 2027.
- **Hoping for a big jump before you sell?** This forecast doesn't support waiting for one.
