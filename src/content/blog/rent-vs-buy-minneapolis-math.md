**"Renting is throwing money away" isn't the whole story, but it's closer to the truth than people think. In the video above I run the real numbers on a typical Minneapolis home, the way most people actually live, and show when buying comes out ahead, and when renting really is the smarter move.**

Every figure below is from the video and was current as of November 2026, when I made it. This isn't financial advice: talk to a lender, a financial advisor or your tax pro about your own situation.

## The two numbers that drive everything

- **Price:** in August, the median sales price in the city of Minneapolis was just under **$370,000** (Minneapolis Area REALTORS®). Across the 16-county metro it was a little over $400,000. I use the city number.
- **Rate:** as of October 1, Freddie Mac's average 30-year fixed rate was about **7.3%**.

Good news for buyers first: in August there were about **1,050 homes for sale** in Minneapolis, up about 9% from a year earlier, a little over a three-month supply. Homes took about **53 days** to sell and sellers got about **98.5%** of list price. In plain English, you can negotiate a little: an inspection repair or help with closing costs. Just don't build your budget around getting it.

## What a Minneapolis home costs every month

Buying at about **$368,000** with 20% down (about $74,000), plus closing costs and Minnesota's mortgage registration tax, is a little over **$85,000 in cash** on day one. Then each month:

| Cost | Per month |
|---|---|
| Principal and interest at 7.3% | about $2,000 |
| Property taxes (current Hennepin County rates) | about $450 |
| Homeowners insurance (Minnesota average near $4,000 a year as of June, per Insurify) | about $330 |
| Maintenance (1% of value a year) | about $300 |
| **Total** | **about $3,100** |

That's with no HOA, no utilities and no mortgage insurance.

## What the same house costs to rent

Don't compare a house payment to a one-bedroom apartment. Apartment List puts the median **two-bedroom** rent in Minneapolis at about **$1,700**, with rents up about 3% year over year. For a **three-bedroom single-family house** across the metro, Rentometer put the median at **$2,500** earlier this year. That's the fair comparison.

On the surface: about $3,100 to own vs. $2,500 to rent, a **$600 gap** in renting's favor. This is where most people stop doing the math, and where they get it wrong.

## Compare the money you never get back

Every dollar of rent is gone the day you pay it. For an owner, part of the payment is **principal**, which is your money, like a forced savings account. In year one on this home, about **$240 a month** goes to principal and about **$1,800** to interest. Add taxes, insurance and maintenance, and the money an owner never gets back is about **$2,900 a month** vs. **$2,500** for the renter. So the real gap is closer to **$400**, and it shrinks every year as more of your payment goes to principal and rent keeps rising.

## Your payment is locked. Rent isn't.

If rent rises about 3% a year, that $2,500 house rents for a little over **$2,900 by year seven**, and the renter has paid about **$230,000** over those seven years. The owner's principal and interest stays about $2,000 a month in year 7, year 15 and year 30 unless they refinance. Taxes and insurance can still go up, but the biggest piece is fixed.

## What the buyer walks away with

Using a modest **2.6% a year** appreciation (how much the Twin Cities metro median rose in 2025, per Minnesota REALTORS®):

- **After 5 years:** worth about $418,000, loan down to $278,000, about **$140,000 in equity**
- **After 7 years:** worth about $440,000, loan about $270,000, about **$170,000 in equity**

Selling costs about 7% (commissions, which are negotiable, closing costs and Minnesota's deed tax), and you still walk away with real wealth. Want to see the full list? Read [what it really costs to sell a Twin Cities home](/blog/cost-to-sell-a-home-twin-cities).

In my side-by-side comparison, where the renter spends the monthly difference on life rather than investing it, the buyer is ahead by about **$5,000 after 5 years**, **$23,000 after 7** and **$53,000 after 10**.

## What changes the answer

- **The rent you'd really pay:** if the comparable house rents closer to $3,000, buying pulls ahead in about 5 years.
- **The rate:** at about 6.5%, the payment drops about $150 a month and buying pulls ahead around year nine; at 6%, around year seven.
- **Negotiation:** with more homes on the market, a seller credit or repair puts real money back in your pocket.
- **Watch out for:** an older home that needs a roof or furnace can push maintenance toward 2% a year and wipe out much of the advantage, which is why a good inspection matters. And the mayor's proposed 2027 Minneapolis budget includes an **11.3% property tax levy increase**; it was a proposal, not final, with the budget expected in December. Build in some room either way.

## When renting really wins

**Rent and invest** is a real strategy: put the down payment in the market and invest the monthly difference. At about 5% a year, never skipping a month, the renter can stay ahead for a long time at a 7.3% rate. With 2.6% appreciation, buying doesn't pull ahead until around **year 15 or 16**, and if prices grow closer to 1% a year, renting can win for 20 years or more. The catch: it only works if you actually invest every month for years, and most people don't.

Renting also makes sense if you'll be here **less than about 5 years**, or your job or plans might change. And if a $1,700 two-bedroom apartment is all you need, renting and investing won in every scenario I ran. No shame in that. It's just math.

## Three questions to ask yourself

1. How sure am I that I'll still be in the Twin Cities 10 years from now?
2. If I keep renting, will I really invest the difference, or spend it?
3. Can I handle a surprise repair without wrecking my budget?

If you answered "pretty sure," "probably not" and "yes," buying is probably your move. If you went the other way, renting for now is a smart decision, not a failure. I didn't include tax deductions because they depend on your situation; talk to a tax professional.
