**Should you buy a brand-new home or an older one? There's no single right answer. After decades in the Twin Cities market, I've watched buyers fall in love with both. Here's the honest comparison you'll want before you commit.**

## Older homes: what you get

**Character.** Minneapolis and Saint Paul have some of the best historic homes in the Midwest: Craftsman, Tudor Revival and mid-century homes with charm a new build can't match. Think grand staircases, wavy leaded-glass windows, floors they don't make anymore, and yes, laundry chutes.

**Location.** These homes sit close to downtown, the lakes and the best food in the city. The Chain of Lakes alone gives you miles of walking and biking trails next to many older neighborhoods.

**Walkability.** Neighborhoods like Linden Hills, parts of Longfellow and Northeast were built for walking. Linden Hills scores around 65 for walkability and sits right next to Lake Harriet.

**Trees.** Fifty-plus-year-old neighborhoods have canopies over the street that take decades to grow.

**A deep, proven buyer pool.** People have always wanted to live in these neighborhoods, and that demand is steady.

## Older homes: the trade-offs

- **Insulation and air sealing.** Our winters find older homes' weak spots, which can add up on heating bills.
- **Ice dams.** Light attic insulation and poor ventilation let heat escape and melt roof snow, which refreezes at the edge and backs water under the shingles.
- **Knob-and-tube wiring** in homes built before about 1950. It has no ground, and many insurers want it replaced before they'll cover the home. Home warranties won't cover it either.
- **No central air.** Many older homes use a boiler and radiators, and adding central air to a whole house gets pricey.
- **Sewer lines**, some over 100 years old. Always scope them. A full replacement from house to street can run roughly **$12,000 to $25,000** (the last one I saw was about $15,000); lining can cost about half if there aren't big voids.
- **Detached garages.** Come winter, many buyers wish they had an attached one.

Budget for surprises, and never skip the inspection. (More in [6 red flags in older homes](/blog/old-minneapolis-homes-red-flags).)

## New construction: what you get

**Everything new, under warranty**, with lower maintenance early on and no decades of surprises behind the walls.

**Built for our climate.** Minnesota's building standards are stricter than many states', because our winters demand it.

**Minnesota's statutory warranty**: one year on workmanship and materials, two years on plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling systems, and ten years on major structural defects. Many people miss this: it **transfers to the next owner** if you sell.

**Energy efficiency.** Tighter walls, modern HVAC and properly insulated attics mean lower heating bills and virtually no ice dams. No knob-and-tube, no old sewer lines, and central air is a given.

**Modern layouts**: open floor plans, higher ceilings and bigger primary suites.

**Builder incentives.** One Twin Cities builder recently advertised rates as low as **4.875%** plus closing cost help, and incentives nationally hit a five-year high last year. Many incentives require the builder's lender, so have your agent compare an outside lender's offer, because savings can hide a higher fee elsewhere. (See [builders slashing prices](/blog/builders-cutting-prices-twin-cities).)

## New construction: what to check

- **Location.** Most new construction is in outer-ring suburbs like Maple Grove, Woodbury and Lakeville, often 15 to 25-plus minutes from downtown. If you work in the suburbs or from home, that may not matter.
- **Builder quality varies**, even within the same suburb, so research the specific builder.
- **Hidden costs.** Landscaping, a deck, blinds and window coverings can add **$30,000 to $50,000**, and you won't always get that back. Spend $50,000 and it might add only about $25,000 in value.
- **Early resale.** Sell within two or three years in a development that's still building, and you're competing with the builder's new inventory and incentives.
- **Character takes time.** Mature trees and a settled feel take years, even decades.

## The question nobody asks: your daily drive

How do you feel about your commute in January? Lakeville to downtown is about 25 miles, roughly 30 minutes in light traffic, but rush hour or fresh snow can stretch it toward 45 minutes each way. **Test the drive at real commute times**, ideally on a winter morning, before you commit.

## How to decide

- **Want character, walkability and closeness to the city?** Older homes often win.
- **Want energy savings, low early upkeep and builder deals?** New construction may win.
- **Selling in about three years?** Lean toward resale, so you won't compete with a builder when you sell.
- **Staying three to five years or more?** New construction can make a lot of sense as the community grows up around you.
