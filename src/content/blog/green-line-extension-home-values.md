**Minnesota just spent about $2.86 billion, not on a stadium or a highway, but on 14.5 miles of light rail. It's the most expensive public project in state history, and a lot of people are angry about it. I'll be straight with you about the facts, the problems, and what the numbers say about homes near the stations.**

Details are as of my video above (June 2026).

## What was built

The **Green Line Extension** (Southwest LRT) runs **14.5 miles** from downtown Minneapolis to Eden Prairie, through St. Louis Park, Hopkins and Minnetonka, with **16 new stations**. The ride from Eden Prairie to downtown takes about 30 minutes, one seat with no transfers.

## Why people are upset

- The original budget was **$1.25 billion**, with opening planned for 2023.
- Tunneling problems, pandemic delays, design changes and cost overruns pushed the total to **about $2.86 billion**, more than double, and opening to **2027**, four years late. The Office of the Legislative Auditor, a nonpartisan watchdog, documented it.
- As of early 2026, it was over 90% complete, with test trains running and more than 4,000 safety tests to finish. No opening month had been set.

My honest take: many people in these communities never wanted it. At roughly $200 million per mile, it's expensive. Metro Transit reported about 46.5 million total rides in 2024 (up about 6%), still below pre-pandemic levels. Most suburban residents along the line drive and will keep driving; for many commuters, rail won't save much time. And the line has missed multiple opening targets, so if you're making a real estate decision based on 2027, build in some cushion.

## What the numbers say anyway

The Blue Line opened in 2004, so there are about 20 years of data. Research on home prices near light rail is genuinely mixed, but Minneapolis Area Realtors found homes near Blue Line stations sold for about **$220,000**, compared with about **$194,000** for similar homes farther away.

And the number that's hard to argue with: about **$3 billion in private investment** is already planned or permitted within a 10-minute walk of the line, before a single passenger boards. Developers don't commit that kind of money on a guess. I can question whether $2.86 billion was the right call and still respect what the market is saying. Both are true.

## Station by station

- **[St. Louis Park](/communities/st-louis-park)** (Louisiana Avenue, Wooddale Avenue, Beltline Boulevard) is already one of the hottest inner-ring suburbs, with strong appreciation, walkability and the West End. Rail access widens the pool of future buyers.
- **[Hopkins](/communities/hopkins)** (Blake Road, Downtown Hopkins, Shady Oak) has been called the most underrated suburb in the metro for years, and I agree: a walkable downtown with breweries, restaurants, new development and attainable prices. Rail to downtown is brand new here, and projects like the Moline near the downtown station show developers are betting on it.
- **[Minnetonka](/communities/minnetonka)**'s main stop serves the **Opus** business area, one of the busiest employment hubs in the west metro. Connecting it to downtown in about 30 minutes is the part of this line that makes the most practical sense to me.
- **[Eden Prairie](/communities/eden-prairie)** (SouthWest, Town Center, Golden Triangle, City West): buyers here have cars and freeways and mostly won't commute by train. But having the option still expands the buyer pool when you sell. More in [Eden Prairie will never be the same](/blog/eden-prairie-light-rail-redevelopment).

## For buyers

Value tied to transit tends to build before opening day. Once trains carry passengers, much of the opportunity has passed, and while it's still in testing with no opening month, most buyers aren't factoring it in yet.

But **don't buy near a station just for the train.** The home, the neighborhood and your budget come first. If you're already shopping in these cities, treat the line as an extra factor, not the whole picture.

## For sellers

If you own near one of these 16 stations, you have a marketing story that didn't exist before. A buyer who works downtown can now seriously consider your home without a car commute, and billions in private development bring more shops, dining and walkability.
