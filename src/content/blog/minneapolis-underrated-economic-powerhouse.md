**Say "economic powerhouse" and most people think New York, San Francisco or Chicago. Almost nobody says Minneapolis. If you own a home here or plan to buy one, that blind spot matters, because the economy underneath this market is a big part of why Twin Cities homes have held their value.**

Figures are as cited in my video above (spring 2026).

## Who Minneapolis really is

The Minneapolis–Saint Paul metro is home to **15 Fortune 500 headquarters**, more per capita than any of the country's 30 largest metros. The list includes **UnitedHealth Group, Target, Best Buy, 3M, General Mills, U.S. Bancorp, Ameriprise Financial, Hormel, Xcel Energy and Ecolab**. Add **Cargill**, the largest privately held company in the U.S., and you have global companies with hundreds of thousands of employees. Minnesota's GDP hit a record of about **$395 billion** in 2024.

## The five pillars under your home's value

### 1. A headquarters economy

When a Fortune 500 company does well, the ripple effect is huge: accountants, IT staff, marketing teams, vendors and restaurants all benefit. Fifteen of them in one metro means steady, high-wage demand for housing, which is very different from a one-industry town.

### 2. A diverse economy

Health care, medical devices, retail, finance, food production, energy, manufacturing, education and technology don't all move together. When one has a bad year, the others keep going. Minnesota's per-capita income has also been above the national average for decades, which gives the market a deep pool of buyers.

### 3. Medical Alley

The Twin Cities is a global capital of medical devices. **Medtronic** was founded here in 1949, **Boston Scientific** has major operations here, and the region has hundreds of medical device, biotech and life sciences companies. Minnesota was also designated a federal **tech hub** for medical technology. These are high-paying, stable careers, and the people in them buy homes and stay.

### 4. A talent engine

The **University of Minnesota** enrolls over 50,000 students, and dozens more colleges are within an hour's drive. Students graduate and stay because UnitedHealth, Medtronic and Target are hiring. Minnesota also has one of the highest labor-force participation rates in the country.

### 5. Not enough homes

The Twin Cities is structurally undersupplied. In 2025, inventory sat around **two months of supply**, when a balanced market needs four to six. The median sale price reached about **$390,000**, and homes sold for about **98.7% of list price** even with elevated rates. More on supply in [why the Twin Cities housing market froze](/blog/why-twin-cities-housing-market-frozen) and the [inventory update](/blog/minnesota-housing-inventory-7-year-high).

## Industries hiding in plain sight

- **Food and agriculture:** Cargill, General Mills and Hormel. People eat in recessions, and that steadiness shows up in housing.
- **Financial services:** U.S. Bancorp, Ameriprise, Allianz Life, Securian and the **Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis**. That means generations of high-wage jobs and strong lending infrastructure.
- **Health care** keeps growing as the population ages, and Minnesota companies make the pacemakers, stents and insulin pumps that demand runs on.

## How it compares

- **Austin** exploded and then fell sharply (20% or more from its peak), driven more by speculation than a deep economic base.
- **San Francisco** is wealthy but leans heavily on one sector; when tech pulled back, housing took a hit.
- **Miami** has no income tax and warm weather, but it faces rising climate risk and insurance costs.
- **Minneapolis** didn't go parabolic, and it didn't crash. Minnesota also has one of the highest homeownership rates in the country, and people with roots tend to support values over time.

## What 3,600+ sales have taught me

The Twin Cities rewards people who stay, not speculators or flippers. Buyers who choose sound locations and hold for 5, 7 or 10 years have generally built real equity here.
