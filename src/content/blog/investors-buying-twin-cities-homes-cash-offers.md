**If you own a home in the Twin Cities suburbs, you've probably gotten the letter: a company you've never heard of offering to buy your home for cash. Here's what's really going on with investors buying Twin Cities homes, and whether you should take one of those offers. Full disclosure: I own investment homes myself, so I'll give you the whole picture without the scary headlines.**

## The real numbers

"Investor-owned" means the owner has two or more homes they don't live in. As I covered in the video above (August 2026):

- The investor share of Twin Cities single-family homes has more than doubled since 2006, from about **1.5% to about 3.5%**, or roughly **9,800 homes to about 26,000**.
- The biggest player, **Tricon** (part of Blackstone), owns more than **1,300** homes in the metro. In Maple Grove, one firm alone bought close to 30 starter homes.
- Investors first came in after the 2006 to 2008 crash, buying city foreclosures. They returned during the low-rate pandemic years, this time focused on **the suburbs**, and investor buying never fell back to earlier levels.
- They favor homes **built after 1990**, because standard-size doors, windows and parts make them cheaper to maintain.

## Good or bad for the market?

**The concerns:** investors can pull starter homes off the market, first-time buyers can get outbid by cash, and competition can nudge up prices on lower-priced homes. Some owners have no local connection. The biggest worry is fewer first-time buyers getting a shot at owning.

**The other side:** big firms own only a small slice; most investor buying is by local people, including people like me. Investors often buy homes that need repairs first-time buyers can't afford, then fix them up, and they can give sellers a fast, certain option when a home is slow to sell. The biggest firm even says it has recently sold more Minnesota homes than it bought.

Across the whole metro, the effect is small. It's concentrated on **suburban starter homes**, and that's where first homes can be harder to land. Personally, I keep a closer eye on out-of-state firms that treat neighborhoods like spreadsheets; a local owner down the street has a much closer tie to the area. (For more, see [the private equity ban that almost passed](/blog/minnesota-housing-laws-homeowners-2026).)

## If you're a seller with a cash offer

The number is real, it's cash, and the closing date works for you. You're **trading some price for convenience**, and that can make sense if:

- The home needs work you can't or don't want to do
- You need to move quickly or want a sure sale
- You want to avoid showings or carrying two payments

**The catch is price.** These offers usually come in below market value, leaving room for the investor's repairs and profit. **Get a true market value first** so you know the gap. In many cases, listing as-is can still put thousands more in your pocket.

There's also a middle option most sellers never hear about: list on the open market **as-is**, with a flexible closing date and fewer showings, and still aim for top dollar. Don't accept or reject until you've measured the gap. Start with your [home's estimated value](/home-estimate) or our [seller net proceeds calculator](/calculators/seller-net-proceeds).

## If you're a buyer competing with investors

Investors move fast, pay cash and often skip inspections. You can still compete:

1. **Get fully underwritten pre-approval**, not just a pre-qualification letter. It makes your offer feel almost as sure as cash.
2. **Be ready to move within hours** on the right home, and offer flexible terms like a quick close or a move-out date that works for the seller.
3. **Don't fear a home that needs work.** Investors love those homes, and the right loan can help you buy one too.

And remember: you're a real person, and the seller can picture you living there. A big firm can't offer that.
