Drive south on Dodd Boulevard toward Rosemount High School and you will notice the gap before you notice anything else. For three years, the corner where Dodd meets 145th Street held a boarded-up Speedway station, the kind of vacant lot that becomes part of the mental furniture of a commute. Then it just was not there anymore. No gas pumps, no chained doors, no weeds pushing through cracked asphalt. Just a cleared lot at one of the busiest intersections in town, and, more than a year after the demolition crews left, still no sign for what comes next.

That absence of a plan is not a stall. It is the plan.

## What actually happened to that corner

As [Sun Thisweek reported](https://www.hometownsource.com/sun_thisweek/community/rosemount/rosemount-to-purchase-vacant-speedway-gas-station/article_979acccf-c607-4b6a-b08e-196b10188827.html), the Speedway station at 14515 Dodd Boulevard had been tangled in receivership and a lengthy environmental review for nearly three years before the city ever stepped in. Nobody could buy it, fix it, or tear it down while it sat in that limbo, and residents noticed. By the spring of 2025, complaints about the property's condition had reached the City Council and the Rosemount Port Authority, and in May 2025 the Port Authority approved a purchase.

The [city's own announcement](https://www.rosemountmn.gov/CivicAlerts.aspx?AID=604&ARC=1120) laid out the mechanics. The sale closed June 13, 2025, financed through a $300,000 interfund loan from the city's Water Fund, a way for Rosemount to front the money internally and repay itself with interest rather than issue debt. Port Authority Chair Tami Klimpel called it a long-anticipated move: "The property has sat vacant for too long, and our efforts to purchase and transform the space is an important step toward revitalizing a location with tremendous potential." City Administrator Logan Martin said Rosemount was committed to "community pride and progress."

What followed was demolition, confirmed in the city's own newsletter update on the project, and then, notably, nothing. No RFP for a new gas station. No fast food chain announcement. No apartment complex rendering. The lot sits cleared and quiet, and the city has said so itself: this is the first of many planned conversations with the people who live around it, not a done deal waiting on a groundbreaking date.

## Why the city bought first and asked questions second

Here is the part that is easy to miss if you only clocked the demolition and moved on. Rosemount did not wait for a private buyer to decide what that corner becomes. It bought the land itself, giving the city time to work with the community on a plan.

> "Having the Port Authority acquire and control this site allows the city time to work with the community on a plan that reflects the needs of the neighborhood and brings reinvestment to an established part of Rosemount," said Community Development Director Adam Kienberger, as reported by [Sun Thisweek](https://www.hometownsource.com/sun_thisweek/community/rosemount/rosemount-to-purchase-vacant-speedway-gas-station/article_979acccf-c607-4b6a-b08e-196b10188827.html).

That is a deliberate sequencing choice, and it matters because of where this corner sits. It is not out on the edge of a business park. It sits near Rosemount's original townsite, the ground first settled in 1855 by Mary Morrison, a history the city marked with a sign in 2008. The intersection is also flanked by small, independent businesses, Tops Pizza, The Meeting Point, and HD Laundromat among them, and it functions as one of the main routes kids take to get to Rosemount High School. A corner like that gets one real shot at redevelopment before the pattern around it locks in. Rosemount's ownership gives the city a chance to weigh those surroundings before settling on a use.

## The blocks that did not wait around

While the anchor corner sits empty on purpose, the rest of downtown has not been standing still. Two projects moving through the pipeline this year show what kind of momentum is already building just blocks from Dodd and 145th.

- **Lucky Lola Coffee Shop** is planning to open adjacent to The Morrison apartments, with coffee and sundries served inside plus a drive-thru, and a wine bar component built into the concept from the start, according to [Sun Thisweek's year-end business roundup](https://www.hometownsource.com/sun_thisweek/community/rosemount/year-in-review-2025-rosemount-still-a-magnet-for-new-business/article_9e6d1fb0-5b94-42fe-bf21-1d368b87bbea.html).
- **The Copperfield**, an upscale breakfast-lunch-dinner restaurant with an existing location in Mendota Heights, [won unanimous site plan approval](https://www.hometownsource.com/sun_thisweek/community/rosemount/rosemount-planning-commission-approves-site-plan-for-the-copperfields-second-location/article_6072b70b-0507-48a7-973d-2ec8f7661e68.html) from the Rosemount Planning Commission for a second location at 1070 Upper 144th St. W.

Neither of these sits directly on the old Speedway parcel, but both are proof of something the city can point to when it finally does open the conversation about that corner: there is enough foot traffic and enough appetite in this stretch of Rosemount to support a coffee concept with a wine bar and a full-service restaurant expanding out of another suburb entirely. Whatever eventually gets proposed for Dodd and 145th will be judged against a downtown that has already shown it can support a broader mix of businesses.

## Where residents actually got a say

The city has not treated this as a done deal to be announced later. According to Rosemount's project update page, staff and Port Authority members set up a booth, number 12, at Midsummer Faire during last year's Leprechaun Days festival, the same July weekend that brought the town's Grand Day Parade and Blarney Stone Hunt to Central Park in 2025. Residents could stop by, look at a site map, and leave ideas before any concept had been drawn up. The city called it the first of several planned sessions, not the only one.

That order of operations, cleared land first, resident input second, formal proposal last, is unusual enough to be worth noting on its own. Most redevelopment stories in a growing suburb move the other direction: a developer buys the land, submits a plan, and residents react to it at a public hearing after most of the decisions have already been made. Rosemount inverted that sequence for this specific corner, in part because it owns the land outright and answers to nobody's private timeline.

## What this means the next time you're at that light

If you commute past Dodd and 145th, the honest answer right now is that nothing is happening there yet, and that is the point. The city bought itself room to get community input before locking in a use, at a corner where a new tenant, whether another gas station or a drive-through pharmacy, would have quietly reshaped one of the last visible pieces of Rosemount's original downtown for another generation. Meanwhile, the rest of the neighborhood keeps giving the city more evidence to work with. A coffee shop willing to bet on a wine bar concept next to The Morrison. A Mendota Heights restaurant confident enough in the foot traffic to open a second location a few blocks away.
