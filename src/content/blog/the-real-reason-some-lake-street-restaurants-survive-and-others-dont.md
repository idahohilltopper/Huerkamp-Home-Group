Walk past 771 East Lake Street this month and you'll see workers applying window film to a storefront that spent years as Wuollet Bakery. By the time the leaves turn, it will be Rustica, the Minneapolis bakery known for sourdough and laminated pastry, moving into a location that has essentially been baked goods real estate for a generation. Two blocks away, a different kind of transition just played out with a much rougher ending: Stalk & Spade, the vegan burger spot that opened to long lines and real critical acclaim, closed its doors as of November 1.

Same street. Same season of change. Very different outcomes. If you live in or around Wayzata and you've been trying to figure out why some Lake Street concepts seem to glide into permanence while others vanish within a year or two, the answer isn't really about the food. It's about who's backing the concept, how much patience the space demands, and whether the city itself will even let you operate the way you want to. This stretch of Lake Street is one of the most expensive, most watched half-miles of retail in the west metro, and this summer it's been unusually clear about what it takes to last there.

## A Legacy Bakery Space Doesn't Stay Empty Long

The Wuollet Bakery site was never going to sit vacant. It's on Lake Street, a block from the water, in a town that treats its bakery-cafe as a civic amenity as much as a business. Rustica is a known quantity from the Twin Cities culinary scene, arriving with its signature sourdough, kouign-amann, and an established coffee program already built out elsewhere. That's the safest kind of bet a landlord on this corridor can make: a proven operator inheriting a location that already trained the neighborhood to walk in for a morning pastry.

Compare that to what happened a few blocks over at Josefina, where the coffee and pastry counter (serving Patisserie 46 pastries) opened at 8 a.m. daily and drew a line down Lake Street on a recent Saturday morning. That's not a new concept finding its footing. That's an already-successful restaurant adding a low-risk extension to capture morning foot traffic it was already generating. The pattern repeats: the safest moves on Lake Street come from operators who already have a foothold, not first-time concepts betting everything on one storefront.

## Critical Acclaim Isn't Enough

Stalk & Spade's closure is the clearest evidence that praise doesn't pay the rent on this street. Lines out the door and glowing reviews weren't enough to keep a single-concept vegan burger restaurant open past its first couple of years in a location where rent, staffing, and seasonal foot traffic swing hard between May and September.

Eloise took a different path rather than close outright. Ownership listened to feedback that the restaurant had drifted too far toward "special occasion" territory, and it's transforming into the more casual Bar Eloise, with the old intimate patio rebuilt into a full lakeside dining space that now takes both individual reservations and full buyouts. That's a business reading the room and changing its offer instead of waiting to be pushed out.

Here's what's shifted on this stretch of Lake Street in the past year, in plain terms:

- **Closed:** Stalk & Spade (vegan burgers), after a strong opening run
- **Transforming:** Eloise, becoming the more casual Bar Eloise with an expanded lakeside patio
- **Incoming:** Rustica, taking over the former Wuollet Bakery space at 771 E. Lake Street
- **Recently opened:** Macanda, a Latin-Japanese concept on the lakeshore, and its attached HiFi vinyl lounge
- **Recently opened:** PuraLima Cantina, which opened its third Twin Cities location on Lake Street on July 13

Two closures or pivots, three new footholds, all within a walkable stretch of the same street. That's not decline. It's a market sorting itself in real time, and the businesses with either deep local roots or an established parent brand are the ones coming out ahead.

## The Backers Who Keep Winning

Macanda is the clearest example of who tends to win this game. It's the work of Daniel del Prado and Aaron Switz, the same team behind Josefina, applying a proven playbook (a strong existing following, an established operating team, capital to build out a design-driven space) to a brand-new concept on Lake Minnetonka's shoreline. That's meaningfully different from an independent operator opening their first restaurant cold.

PuraLima Cantina followed the opposite but equally telling logic. Its Wayzata location isn't a bet on an unproven idea, it's a third Twin Cities location for a build-your-own burrito and bowl concept that already knows its unit economics elsewhere. Grand opening free entrees until 1:30 p.m. and $5 margaritas the following day aren't just promotions, they're the confident moves of a brand that's done this math before.

The lesson for anyone who eats on this street regularly: when a new name shows up on Lake Street, the first question worth asking isn't "is the food good," it's "who's actually behind this, and have they done it before." That answer predicts survival better than a review does.

## How City Policy Shapes Patio Plans

City policy is another factor shaping activity along Lake Street. In April, the council held a workshop reviewing a sketch plan from CoV restaurant, which wanted to add a small outdoor bar within its existing patio footprint on Lake Street, part of which sits on city right-of-way. The city's code currently prohibits outdoor bars in the right-of-way, and council members were split on whether to change that. Council members discussed precedent, downtown aesthetics, and preserving what several members called a family-friendly atmosphere. The council didn't reach consensus to draft a formal amendment, and the applicant was told a path forward might be modifying the plan to keep the bar entirely on private property instead.

The workshop illustrates how city review can shape proposals for outdoor dining in Wayzata.

## Thirty Years Is the Real Benchmark

If new concepts are the experiment, Gianni's Steakhouse is the control group. The restaurant, described as the "crown jewel of Lake Street," was recently recognized by the Chamber for consistency, an honor that surprised even the group giving it out. As Sarah Keller, speaking for the Chamber, put it:

> "There were a number of us on the nominating committee who said, 'Surely they've won before.'"

They hadn't. Owner Terri Huml-Fox accepted the recognition as the restaurant approaches 30 years in business, saying the honor reflects "the hard work and commitment of our entire team." That kind of longevity, on a street where restaurants routinely turn over in one or two seasons, is the real proof point of what durability looks like here. It's not flash. It's a family-run steakhouse that has weathered the trend cycles that have turned over restaurants a few doors down.

## The Street's Biggest Stress Test Is About a Month Away

All of this turnover is happening in the run-up to the one weekend that puts every business on Lake Street to a real test at once. The 52nd Annual James J. Hill Days returns September 11 through 13, 2026, presented by Sandvold Financial Group, with over 175 street market vendors and the Rails & Ales Craft Beer Festival kicking things off that Friday evening. The [official festival page](https://jamesjhilldays.wayzatawestmetrochamber.com/) confirms the full weekend lineup, and past years give a sense of scale: the 2025 event, the 51st annual, drew more than 80,000 guests over three days, according to [KARE11's coverage](https://www.kare11.com/article/news/local/kare11-saturday/james-j-hill-days-returns-festivities-and-live-music-at-lake-minnetonka/89-154e207d-21d2-4fbc-8789-5850526f9c8e). [Explore Minnesota](https://www.exploreminnesota.com/event/james-j-hill-days/10710) lists the same three-day span with live music, food trucks, and the parade that's closed out the festival since 1975.

For every restaurant and shop on this stretch, that weekend is the single highest-traffic test of the year, and it lands right after a summer that already reshuffled who's operating where. Rustica will barely have its doors open. Macanda and PuraLima will be facing their first Hill Days crowds. Bar Eloise will find out if its more casual pivot pulls in festival foot traffic the old Eloise never chased. Gianni's will just keep doing what it's done for three decades.

If you live near this stretch of [Wayzata](/communities/wayzata), the churn on Lake Street this year isn't a sign that downtown is struggling. It's a sign the street is expensive and visible enough that only the concepts with real backing, a clear read on what the neighborhood wants, or three decades of trust actually stick.
