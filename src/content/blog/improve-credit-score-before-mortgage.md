**A higher credit score can mean a better mortgage rate. I sat down with Jeff, a loan officer at Granite Bank with about 25 years in lending, to walk through seven ways to improve your score before you buy, and what to do if you have no score at all.**

General guidance as discussed in my video above (2025). Every borrower's situation is different, so talk with a lender before making changes.

## How lenders read your credit

There are three credit bureaus: **Equifax, Experian and TransUnion**. Mortgage lenders pull a "tri-merge" report and typically use your **middle score**. With scores of 700, 680 and 650, you'd qualify off the 680.

## 1. Pay your bills on time

Payment history is about **35%** of your score. Mortgages report monthly; credit cards and installment loans vary, and some report less often. A payment generally isn't reported late to the bureaus until it's **30 days past due**, though you'll usually owe a late fee sooner (mortgages typically have a 10- to 15-day grace period). Don't play it close: just pay on time.

## 2. Keep credit card balances low

Credit utilization is how much of your revolving credit you're using. Aim to keep balances **under 30%** of your limits; scores tend to dip above 50% and drop further above 80%. On a $5,000 limit, that means staying under about $1,500. Mortgages and car loans are judged mainly on payment history, not utilization.

## 3. Don't open new credit while you're buying

New accounts and inquiries can signal that you need credit. The impact varies with your overall credit profile, but the rule of thumb holds: **check with your lender before you apply for anything**. I once had clients near their debt-to-income limit buy furniture on "0% financing, delivered after closing." It still showed up. They had to return the furniture and delay closing.

### Rapid rescores

If you're close to a better tier, your lender can run a credit "what-if" tool that shows exactly what would raise your scores, often something as simple as paying down a specific card. Once you pay it and send proof, the lender can request a **rapid rescore**, usually within days, without a new hard pull. Jeff had a client with strong income but a score under 600 who paid about **$2,300** across four cards and reached about 630, enough to qualify for FHA financing.

## 4. Pay down existing debt

Lenders look at **debt-to-income (DTI)**: your new housing payment plus monthly debts on your credit report, divided by your **gross** monthly income. Ideally, keep it under about **43%**. Automated approvals can sometimes go to about 50% for conventional loans and higher for FHA. Utilities don't count.

Paying off debt isn't always the best use of cash, though. A car loan with fewer than about 10 payments left often isn't counted, and sometimes paying a card down to a specific balance helps more than paying it off. Let your lender analyze it first. A good loan officer also asks what payment you're **comfortable** with, not just what you qualify for. Try our [affordability calculator](/calculators/affordability).

## 5. Check for and dispute errors

Errors are common: an account from a past marriage, a "closed" card with a forgotten $1 balance that went late, or a judgment belonging to someone with a similar name. Jeff once found a **$500,000 judgment** that wasn't the client's; once it was removed, their score jumped from about 530 to nearly 800, and they bought a home within months.

Get your free reports from all three bureaus at **AnnualCreditReport.com**, the official free site. If something's wrong, **dispute it in writing**; the bureau must verify it, typically within 30 days, or remove it. Take app-based scores (like Credit Karma) with a grain of salt; they can be well off from what a lender sees. A **soft pull** doesn't affect your score; a **hard pull** is what a lender uses for a full pre-approval.

## 6. Become an authorized user (carefully)

If you have little credit history, being added as an authorized user on a responsible family member's long-standing card can bring that history onto your report, though it can take a few months. It works in reverse too: being an authorized user on a maxed-out card can drag you down, and removing yourself can help.

## 7. Keep old accounts open

Paid-off old cards help your score by adding available credit and history length. Closing them can raise your utilization overnight. I closed an old department store card I'd had for 20 years, and my score took a hit that took about six months to recover.

## No credit score? You may still be able to buy

You don't necessarily need a credit score to buy a home. **Manual underwriting** can approve borrowers using rent, utilities, phone and insurance payment history, along with savings and a solid down payment, often at a slightly higher rate. Self-employed buyers whose tax returns understate cash flow may qualify through a bank's **portfolio loan** or a **bank-statement (non-QM) loan**.
