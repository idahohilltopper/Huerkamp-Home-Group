**The fastest way to lose $60,000 in Minneapolis is to buy the wrong older home. Most buyers walk into these problems without knowing it. Here are six red flags in older Twin Cities homes, what they can cost, and how to spot them before you make an offer.**

Older homes can be great investments, but only when you know what you're buying. Costs below are as of my video above (January 2026).

## 1. Fieldstone and rubble foundations

Picture a cute 1910 home in Longfellow with hardwood floors and crown molding, sitting on a fieldstone foundation that's slowly falling apart. These foundations are common in homes built **before about 1920**, in neighborhoods like Seward, Longfellow, Summit Hill, Powderhorn and parts of Northeast. They're literally stones from fields held together with mortar, and Minnesota's freeze-thaw cycles break that mortar down. Stones shift, and walls can push inward.

**Watch for:** freshly painted basement walls (paint can hide cracks and white, chalky efflorescence), bowed walls, loose stones, seepage, musty smells and cracks wider than a quarter inch. Waterproofing won't fix structural failure.

**Cost:** major repairs can run **$20,000 to $60,000 or more**. Tuckpointing runs about **$4,000 to $8,000**. Adding drain tile and a sump pump relieves water pressure on the walls. **Get a structural engineer's report before you buy.** I own a couple of fieldstone-foundation properties myself without issues, because I did that homework up front.

## 2. Aging boilers and radiators

Some Minneapolis homes still run boilers installed when horses shared the road with Model Ts. They're common in homes built 1900 to 1940, in Uptown, Lynnhurst, Tangletown and parts of Saint Paul.

- Replacing an old boiler with a modern high-efficiency unit can cost **$10,000 to $20,000 or more**.
- Old radiators can leak and cause hidden rot and mold.
- With no ductwork, adding central air to a multi-story home can run **$12,000 to $25,000**. Ductless mini-splits are often a better fit, at about **$3,000 to $6,000 per zone**.

**What to do:** check the boiler's age (over 30 years likely means replacement soon) and hire a boiler specialist for pressure, leak and combustion tests. General inspections often miss internal corrosion.

## 3. Drainage and grading problems

Water is the silent destroyer, and a single spring snowmelt can flood your basement and your budget. It's worse on low lots and older homes where soil has settled. Water that runs toward the house soaks the soil, then freezes and pushes on the foundation. Damage can run from about **$5,000 to $70,000**.

**Watch for:** soil sloping toward the house, standing water after rain, missing downspout extensions, missing or undersized gutters, and soggy or icy spots by the foundation.

**The fix:** regrade so the ground drops at least **6 inches over the first 10 feet**, extend downspouts 10 to 15 feet out, and add gutters (about **$1,200 to $4,000** for a midsize home).

## 4. Knob-and-tube wiring

Insurance companies' answer to knob-and-tube wiring is often "find another company." It's common in homes built before about 1930, in Powderhorn, Seward, Whittier and older parts of Saint Paul. It has no ground and no modern safety features, and it was never designed for today's electrical loads. Insurers are denying or limiting coverage even with small amounts, and partial updates that tie old circuits into new ones are dangerous.

**Cost:** a full rewire typically runs **$15,000 to $25,000 or more**, plus plaster and wall repairs. I've done it on homes I've renovated.

**What to do:** get a written inspection from a licensed electrician (including attic and basement), ask for permits and signoffs that prove updates were done, and **confirm you can get insurance before you waive contingencies**.

## 5. Roofs not built for Minnesota snow

Roofs built before about 1960, homes with complex gables, and flat roofs on some older duplexes and fourplexes are more vulnerable to heavy snow, sagging, cracked rafters and **ice dams**, which push water under shingles and cause rot, mold and ceiling leaks. It's common in Uptown, Whittier and parts of Saint Paul.

**Watch for:** sagging, curling shingles, soft spots, obvious patches and ceiling stains. If the roof is under snow, check permits for its age.

**Know the standards:** Minnesota energy code calls for about **R-49** attic insulation (roughly 16 to 20 inches), good ventilation, and ice-and-water shield along the eaves. A full roof replacement on an older home can run **$12,000 to $25,000 or more**.

**Bonus tip:** a north-facing driveway gets little sun and can turn into a skating rink all winter.

## 6. Basement moisture, radon and DIY finishes

Minnesota ranks in the top five states for radon, a colorless, odorless gas linked to lung cancer that can be in any home, new or old. The test adds about **$300** to your inspection, and mitigation typically costs about **$1,700 to $5,000**. Action is recommended at **4.0 pCi/L** or higher. Sellers must disclose known test results, but testing isn't required, so test.

Every Minnesota home has some mold; the question is whether levels are high. If your inspector sees signs, a mold test compares indoor and outdoor air samples.

**Watch for:** white powder on walls, musty smells, water stains, warped flooring, and no drain tile or sump pump. A good drain tile system with a sealed sump is a big green flag. Be wary of fresh paint, new carpet or DIY paneling, which can hide problems. Tile, sealed concrete and vinyl are the best basement floors.

## What this means in today's market

When I made the video, pre-1940 homes with original systems were selling in the low to mid-$300,000s, while fully updated ones averaged about **$420,000 to $525,000**. Homes with unresolved foundation, electrical or boiler problems sit longer and sell at real discounts; updated homes with proof of the work were selling in about 20 days.

That gap is the opportunity. With the right pros (structural engineers, electricians, boiler specialists, roofers and radon contractors), you can find great deals on character homes that are actually smart buys.
