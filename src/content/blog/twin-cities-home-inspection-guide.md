**I sat down with Andy, owner of Everest Home Inspections and a former builder and remodeler who has done thousands of inspections, to walk through everything inspection-related: the types of inspections, what he checks room by room, and the issues that get expensive.**

Costs are rough figures as discussed in my video above (2025). We're not electricians, plumbers or HVAC pros, so get licensed trades to quote any repair.

## The types of inspections

- **Radon.** Radon is a radioactive gas from the soil and the second leading cause of lung cancer in the U.S. The Minnesota Department of Health estimates about **2 in 5** Minnesota homes have elevated levels. The test takes about two days. The EPA action level is 4 pCi/L; my own house tested around 19. A mitigation system typically runs about **$2,000 to $3,000** on an average home.
- **Pre-inspection.** In multiple-offer situations, some buyers have a quick, roughly 45-minute look at the major components (roof, furnace, electrical panel) before writing an offer.
- **Sewer scope.** Worth doing on almost any home. Older lines in Minneapolis, Saint Paul, Richfield and Bloomington are often **clay** or **Orangeburg** (a tar-paper pipe used around World War II), and tree roots love the cracks. Even new homes can have a pipe crushed by a rock during final grading.
- **Well water.** The four common tests are **coliform bacteria, nitrates, arsenic and lead**. Some government-backed loans, like VA and USDA, often require one.
- **Septic.** Have any system over about **20 years** old inspected; some counties require it at sale. Older drain-field systems (no mound) are frequently non-compliant unless the soil is very sandy, and a new system can run **$35,000 to $45,000**. See [septic, radon and wells](/blog/minnesota-homes-hidden-costs-septic-radon-wells).
- **Stucco testing.** Inspectors drill small holes at window corners, roof-wall joints and other trouble spots, and probe for moisture and soft sheathing. Homes built roughly **1990 to 2005** are the riskiest: houses got tighter while flashing often wasn't done well. Repair bills on some homes have run into the **hundreds of thousands**. We had a North Oaks client with no visible signs whose bids came back at $175,000 to $400,000+.

## Outside

- **Driveway and apron:** cracks are normal in Minnesota, but a settled apron in front of the garage can let water and salt undermine the garage foundation.
- **Roof:** missing shingles, worn rubber boots around plumbing vents, **kick-out flashing** (common only since about 2006–2007) where roofs meet walls, and hail damage. A few hail marks don't automatically mean a bad roof.
- **Siding:** **Masonite** (common from the 1970s to around 1990) holds up if kept painted and away from the ground and roof lines. Newer engineered siding like LP SmartSide comes pre-finished with a long warranty.
- **Grading and gutters:** soil should slope away from the house. Good drainage and clean gutters prevent a lot of basement water problems.
- **Sidewalks:** cracking is normal; it's a concern when it becomes a trip hazard, like a settled walk that turns a step into a 10-inch rise. Fixes range from replacement to mudjacking or foam lifting.

## Inside, top to bottom

- **Attic:** bath fans should vent outside (not into the attic) through insulated ducts, or condensation can lead to mold. Inspectors also look for leaks, ventilation problems, cracked trusses and rodents. Mice, squirrels or bats in insulation can mean removing and replacing it.
- **Bedrooms:** ceiling stains, cracks, outlets, windows (operation, broken seals, cracked glass) and alarms. In Minnesota, you need a **smoke alarm in every bedroom and on every level**, and a **carbon monoxide alarm within 10 feet** of each bedroom. Replacing windows ranges from about $7,500 on a simple rambler to well over $100,000 on a large two-story.
- **Drywall cracks:** cracks in vaulted ceilings often come from trusses moving with temperature swings. Hairline cracks are usually cosmetic; wider ones deserve a closer look, and possibly a structural engineer.
- **Bathrooms:** GFCI outlets, leaks and toilets that are actually secured to the floor (many aren't). Older homes may have **S-traps** instead of vented P-traps; venting them to Minnesota code can be a bigger job than in Wisconsin, where air admittance valves are allowed.
- **Fireplaces:** a wood-burning fireplace should have a hearth at least 16 inches deep, a sound firebox and a working damper. If you'll actually burn wood in a masonry fireplace, consider a **Level 2 chimney inspection** (a camera up the flue). Liner repairs often run **$5,000 to $10,000**; a quality gas insert is around $6,000.
- **Kitchens and laundry:** GFCI protection, leaks (dishwashers are a common culprit), appliances and cabinets. Dryer vents should be metal and rigid inside walls and ceilings; never vinyl.

## The utility room

- **Electrical panels:** old **fuse boxes** (usually pre-1960) work but take care to fuse correctly. **Pushmatic** breakers are hard to find. **Federal Pacific** and **Zinsco** panels are considered problematic and are worth replacing. Panel replacement that ran $2,000 to $3,000 a few years ago is more like **$5,000 to $10,000** now. New construction now requires whole-panel surge protection, an exterior main disconnect and arc-fault protection on most circuits.
- **Knob-and-tube wiring** (no ground wire) can make a home hard to insure, and rewiring often runs **$10,000 to $15,000+** before patching plaster. See [home insurance tips](/blog/minnesota-home-insurance-premiums-tips).
- **Aluminum branch wiring** (common from about the mid-1940s through the '60s) can loosen and arc at connections.
- **Heating:** most Minnesota homes have forced air. Inspectors check operation, age and condensation leaks; even a brand-new furnace can malfunction. A cracked heat exchanger usually means replacement. Older homes may have **hot-water boilers**; Andy's 1920s boiler still runs.
- **Water heaters:** gravity-vented models need a vent that rises properly (a taller replacement tank can break that). **Tankless** units need annual descaling.

## Foundations

- **Poured concrete:** what Andy and I would both choose. Builders generally don't consider a crack a defect until it's about **1/4 inch**, though even smaller cracks occasionally leak.
- **Concrete block:** good, especially when core-filled. Watch for horizontal cracks and bowing.
- **Wood:** rare, warmer basements, but a small margin for error and a resale stigma.
- **Brick, limestone and fieldstone:** common in older Minneapolis and Saint Paul homes and farmhouses. Many have stood 130+ years. Limestone needs lime-based mortar and periodic tuckpointing (not hard cement mortar) and tends to seep more.

Whatever the type, **drainage is key**: freeze-thaw cycles push hard on foundations, just like they do on our roads.

## "Past its useful life"

That phrase makes buyers nervous and sellers mad. It doesn't mean something is broken. It means the item (appliances, furnace, water heater, AC) is older than its typical lifespan, so budget for it. Consider a **home warranty or utility service plan** for the first year, typically about $650 to $1,000.
